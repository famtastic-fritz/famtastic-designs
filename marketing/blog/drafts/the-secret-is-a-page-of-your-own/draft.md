# The Secret Is a Page of Your Own

Someone you know starts sending people to an address instead of a profile, and it looks like they found something out. They didn't. They just built one page.

A quiet, recurring moment happens across a lot of trades: a peer notices you have a website now, asks what it's for, and gets a plain answer back. Nothing about the exchange is dramatic, and that's the point of writing it down. There is no secret technique, no growth hack, no result being claimed. There is a page, at an address the owner holds, that didn't exist before.

We built eight short dramatizations of exactly this moment — two pairs of fictional characters, in two different trades, having the same short conversation in their own words. They are illustrative, not real customers, and nothing in them implies a result. What they dramatize is a fact changing, not a business improving. Read them as a way of seeing the moment, not as evidence of anything.

## What actually gets noticed

The observation is never about performance. It's about presence — the fact that an address now exists where there wasn't one. A friend or colleague sees a name and a domain that weren't there before, on a screen you handed them or a card you gave them, and the natural next question is what it actually is.

The honest answer is small on purpose: it's a spot online that's actually owned, not rented from a platform. That is a genuinely different claim than "it's my Linktree" or "it's my booking profile." [The distinction between a real site and a link-in-bio page](https://famtasticdesigns.com/blog/linktree-vs-real-website-what-you-trade-away/) is that one is a page you control, indexed the way a search engine actually indexes pages, sitting at a domain with your name on it — and the other is a list of outbound links pointing at other people's platforms.

## No catch, no hidden tier

When the question turns to price, the honest version is short: one page, a year of hosting, the domain, for $199. [The exact inclusions and boundaries](https://famtasticdesigns.com/blog/199-website-inclusions-and-boundaries/) are the whole bundle stated once — no hidden tier, nothing bundled in that wasn't asked for, and nothing implied beyond what's on that page.

After the first year, the terms are the same regardless of trade or story: $9.99 a month if the hosting continues, plus whatever the domain itself costs to renew. Business email and website maintenance are separate products, sold and priced on their own — never folded into the $199 figure by implication.

## The objection that keeps coming up

The most common pushback isn't about price. It's the assumption that a booking app or marketplace profile already covers this ground. [Who actually owns a client list inside a booking app](https://famtasticdesigns.com/blog/who-owns-your-client-list-booking-app/) is a different question than who can see it, and the same logic applies to the app itself: it hands you access as long as you keep using it, on terms it sets. A page you own works differently — it stays yours whether you keep any particular app or not, and the two were never actually competing for the same job.

## Two conversations, not one script twice

The two pairs of characters below are written as specific people in specific trades, not one script with the details swapped. One pair works a gym floor at dusk; the other talks at a truck tailgate at the end of a shift. The words each pair reaches for are their own — "my own spot online" is not the same sentence as "my own page, one flat price" — because the point is that this observation happens across genuinely different work, not that it happens identically everywhere.

## What we don't claim

Every dramatization ends the same honest way this article does: no revenue figure, no booking count, no ranking, nothing a customer couldn't independently verify. The only numbers that appear anywhere are catalog facts — $199, one page, one year of hosting, the domain, and the renewal terms disclosed alongside them. Nobody in these films is a paying customer, a testimonial, or a real identifiable business, and the characters never claim to be. Every one of them carries the same on-screen line: dramatization, characters are illustrative.
