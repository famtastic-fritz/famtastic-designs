# Linktree vs. a Real Website: What You Trade Away

A link-in-bio page is a good hallway and a poor room. It gets people moving in the right direction, then has nothing for them when they arrive. That is the whole trade, and it is worth looking at honestly rather than being talked out of something that currently works for you.

A **link-in-bio page** is a single page holding a stack of buttons. It solves a narrow problem: most social apps allow one clickable link in a profile, and you have more than one place to send people. Linktree is the best-known version and there are many others. The format is genuinely useful. It is just not a web presence, and the difference costs more than most people expect.

## What the format is actually good at

Start with what is true about it, because plenty is.

It is fast — you can build one in ten minutes with no help. It is free, or nearly free. It is easy to change, which matters when you run one promotion on Tuesday and a different one on Friday. And it solves the one-link problem cleanly, which is exactly what it was built to do. If a bio page is currently routing people to your booking app and your phone number, it is doing a real job. Keep it.

## Trade one: the address

Your page lives at someone else's address with your name attached to the end of it. The middle of a web address — the part between the www and the dot-com — is called the **domain**, and it is the part that belongs to somebody. On a link-in-bio page, that somebody is the platform.

An address in your own name is something you register and renew, and it keeps working regardless of what any company later decides about its free tier, its layout, or its pricing. [What a domain name is and why your business needs one](https://famtasticdesigns.com/blog/what-is-a-domain-name) covers the mechanics without assuming you know any of this already.

## Trade two: the answer

This is the expensive one. A link-in-bio page routes. It does not answer.

Think about what a stranger needs to know before booking you: what you charge, whether you do the specific thing they want, how soon you are free, and where you are. A stack of buttons answers none of it. Every button is a decision the visitor has to make with incomplete information, and every decision is a place they can quietly leave instead.

## Trade three: what is allowed on the page

You get the fields the platform exposes. That is a fair deal when those fields are all you need, and a hard ceiling when they are not.

You cannot add a price list because you decided you should. You cannot add six photographs of the one service you want more of, grouped the way a client actually shops for it. You get buttons, in the order you put them.

## Trade four: who the visitor becomes

On a bio page, a visitor becomes a click. They tap, they leave, and whatever happens next happens on someone else's system.

On a page you own, a visitor can become an inquiry — a name, a service, a rough date, and a way to reach them, sitting in something you keep. That difference compounds quietly. A hundred clicks is a number. A hundred inquiries is a client list.

## Trade five: being findable at all

A link-in-bio page almost never brings you someone who did not already know you existed. It is a destination for people already standing in your profile looking at it.

That one has a mechanism worth understanding properly, and it gets its own article in this series rather than three rushed sentences here.

## What you trade in the other direction

An honest comparison runs both ways. A page of your own costs money — a domain to register and renew, and somewhere to host it. It costs a decision, because a page has to say something specific and you are the one who chooses what.

If you are running something as a side project and you are not trying to be found by strangers, a bio page may honestly be enough for now. That is a real answer and it is sometimes the right one. Nothing here says you are behind.

## The version that actually works

The choice is not really Linktree or a website. It is what sits at the end of the chain.

Keep the bio link. Keep the buttons. Change what one of them points to, so the stranger who taps it lands on a page that belongs to you and answers the question they arrived with. The bio page stays a hallway. It just stops being the whole house.
