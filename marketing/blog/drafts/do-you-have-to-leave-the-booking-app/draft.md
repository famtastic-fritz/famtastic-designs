# Do You Have to Leave the Booking App?

No. And the version of this argument that ends with "so cancel your app" is wrong in a way worth explaining, because it is the reason a lot of people stop reading three paragraphs in.

A channel that brings you paying work is not a problem. If a booking app is filling your calendar, the app is doing its job. The question worth asking is not whether to keep it. It is what happens to your business if it ever stops.

## What each one is actually good at

They're not competing for the same job, which is why "either/or" was always the wrong frame.

- **An app is good at demand you did not create.** Someone opens it already intending to book somebody. You are one of the options. That is real value and it is hard to replicate on your own.
- **A page of your own is good at demand you did create.** Someone heard your name, saw your work, got a referral, or searched for what you specifically do. They need somewhere to land that is yours.

Most businesses have both kinds of demand and only serve one of them. The second kind is the one currently evaporating.

## The one thing worth moving

Not your bookings. The relationship.

Here's the mechanic, plainly. When a client's entire connection to you runs through a platform, the platform holds the thing that makes your business durable: the ability to reach the people who already like your work. If your listing disappears tomorrow — a policy change, a billing lapse, a category reshuffle — the bookings stop, and so does your ability to tell anyone why.

Moving the relationship does not mean pulling anyone off the app or doing anything sneaky with their data. It means that at some point in a visit, in person, the client learns your web address and has a reason to remember it. A card, a mirror decal, a line at the bottom of a receipt. That is the whole mechanism.

## What you actually own

It's a short list, and it's worth being precise about it, because "owning your presence" is the kind of phrase that means nothing until you name the parts.

- **Your domain.** Your address on the web, the thing you register and renew. As long as you renew it, no platform decision can take it.
- **Your page and what is on it.** The words, the photographs, the prices, the order they appear in.
- **The people who contact you directly.** Someone who emails or fills in your form has reached you, not a platform's inbox.

What you do not own, and should not pretend to: reviews written on a platform stay on that platform, and a client who prefers booking through an app will keep booking through the app. Both of those are fine. Neither is a reason to skip having an address of your own.

## How the two feed each other

This is the part people don't expect. The two channels are not neutral toward one another — each makes the other work better.

A client who found you on an app and later looks you up by name finds a real page instead of nothing, which makes the choice they already made feel correct. A stranger who finds your page and prefers the convenience of booking through an app can be sent there, and you have lost nothing. A referral with no handle attached now has somewhere to go.

You end up with one channel you rent and one you own, pointing at each other. That's a considerably stronger position than one channel you rent, which is where this series started.
