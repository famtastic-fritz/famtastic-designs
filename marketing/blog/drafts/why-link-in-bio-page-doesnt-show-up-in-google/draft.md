# Why Your Link-in-Bio Page Doesn't Show Up in Google

It is almost never a setting you missed. A link-in-bio page usually is not hidden from Google and usually is not broken. It simply has almost nothing on it for a search engine to match a search against, and matching is the entire job.

A **link-in-bio page** is the page of buttons you put in a social profile because the app only allows one link. Linktree is the best-known version; the mechanism below applies to all of them.

## The three steps between a page and a search result

These three words get used as if they mean the same thing. They are separate steps, and knowing which one you are stuck at changes what you would do about it.

- **Crawling** is a search engine following links around the web and reading pages, the way a very fast reader with no opinions would.
- **Indexing** is filing a page it has read, so that page can be offered to somebody later. A page never indexed can never appear in any result.
- **Ranking** is choosing which of the filed pages to actually show, for one particular search, in order.

Most people assume the problem is step two. It usually is not — link-in-bio platforms want their pages read and filed, because that is how the platform gets found, so your page is probably in the index right now. You are stuck at step three, for a reason that has nothing to do with settings.

## Ranking is a matching problem, and you gave it nothing to match

A search engine's job is to take the words somebody typed and find the page that best answers them. To do that, it needs words on the page.

Picture your bio page as text with the styling stripped away: your name, "Book Now," "Instagram," "TikTok," "WhatsApp," "Shop," perhaps a line of description. Maybe thirty words. Then picture what a stranger actually types. "Knotless braids near me." "Mobile detailing in Port St. Lucie." "Emergency plumber open Sunday."

None of those words appear on your bio page — not because you did anything wrong, but because the format has no room for them. A page of buttons cannot describe a service, name a neighborhood, quote a price range, or answer a question, and those are the only things a search is made of. There is no query for which a stack of outbound links is the best available answer, so there is no query it wins.

## The second reason: nothing is filed under your name

Every page sits on a **domain** — the middle of the address, between the www and the dot-com. Search engines treat the domain as the identity, and whatever a page earns over time it earns for the domain it sits on.

Your bio page sits on the platform's domain. Every visit and every link pointed at it accrues to the platform's name, because your business name has no address for it to accrue to.

## The exception that fools people

There is one search where your bio page probably does show up: your exact business name. Type it and you may well find the page sitting there.

That feels like proof the system works, and it is worth being clear about why it is not. Searching a business by name is a navigational search — the person already knew you existed and was looking for the door. It brings you nobody new. The searches that grow a business are the ones where nobody typed your name, because nobody knew it yet, and those are the ones a bio page cannot enter.

## So does a link-in-bio page help my SEO?

Asked plainly: it neither helps nor hurts, because there is nothing for it to help.

**SEO** just means the ordinary work of making a page findable in search, and it happens to a page you own. A bio page has no page of yours to pass anything to. If you later build your own site and link to it from your bio, that link mostly earns you visitors rather than search credit — links on social and bio platforms are typically marked in a way that passes no ranking value. Visitors are worth having; just do not expect the link itself to move you up a results page.

## What actually makes a business findable

Two plain things, and nobody can promise you an outcome from either — anyone guaranteeing a ranking is selling something they do not control.

The first is a page on an address in your own name, so there is something for a search engine to file under you rather than under a platform. The second is real text on that page answering what people actually type: what you do, which services specifically, where, roughly what it costs, and what happens when someone contacts you.

That is the whole change — from a page that cannot compete to a page that is eligible. What follows depends on your market and your competition, and it takes time.
