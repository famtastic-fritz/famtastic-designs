# What a Bookable Page Actually Needs

A page that gets you booked is not a prettier version of your app profile. It has a different job, because the person reading it arrived with a different question.

On a marketplace, a visitor has already decided to hire someone from that platform, and is now choosing between the listings in front of them. On your own page, a visitor is deciding something more basic: whether to hire you at all. A page that only lists services answers the first question and skips the second entirely.

## The two things a stranger is deciding

Everything below is in service of these, and anything that serves neither can wait.

- **Can this person do the specific thing I want?** Not "haircuts." The braid pattern, the fade, the gel set, the pre-wedding schedule.
- **Can I trust them with it?** Trust here is mundane. Will they answer, will the price hold, will they be where they said they would be.

Keep those two questions in your head while you read the rest. They are the whole standard.

## Work someone can browse without hunting

A portfolio on a page you own is not a scrapbook. It's the answer to the first question, and it works best when it is organized the way a client shops.

Group by the thing a person would ask for, not by the date you did it. Six clear photographs of the three services you actually want more of will outperform forty photographs of everything you have ever done, because forty photographs make a visitor do the sorting, and visitors don't sort — they leave.

Say what each piece is. "Knotless box braids, mid-back, about five hours" tells a stranger more than a beautiful photograph with no caption, because it quietly answers a scheduling question they had not gotten to yet.

## Services, and the price question

The most common objection to putting prices on a page is that the work varies. That's usually true, and it isn't a reason to say nothing.

A range with a condition attached does the job: "Starting at $X, depending on length and density." A visitor who cannot afford you leaves before taking your time, and a visitor who can stops wondering. Both outcomes are better than the message that says only "how much?"

If you truly can't post a number, post the thing that determines the number. Telling someone what the price depends on is itself an answer.

## One obvious way to start

A page needs exactly one primary action, and it needs to be visible without scrolling on a phone.

That action doesn't have to be a full booking system. A short form asking for the service, a rough date, and a way to reach them is enough to catch someone who would otherwise have closed the tab.

Online scheduling is genuinely better once you have enough inbound to justify it. Add it then — not as the thing you wait for before launching.

Two smaller details do a surprising amount of work here. Say what happens after they submit, in one sentence. And give a second route — a phone number or an email — because some people will never fill in a form.

## The unglamorous details people leave off

These aren't decoration. Each one prevents a specific wasted conversation.

- **Where you are, and whether you travel.** A suite address, a neighborhood, a service radius. This is the single most common reason a promising inquiry dies.
- **When you work.** Even approximately. "Tuesday to Saturday, evenings by arrangement" is enough.
- **Your policies, stated kindly.** Deposits, lateness, cancellation. Written down once, they stop being an awkward conversation.
- **Who you are.** Two or three sentences in your own voice. A stranger is about to sit in your chair for four hours.
