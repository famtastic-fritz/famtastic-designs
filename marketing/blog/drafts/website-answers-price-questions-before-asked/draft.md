# How a Website Answers Price Questions Before They're Asked

Almost none of this is software. The part that does the work is writing an answer down once, in the place where the question keeps getting asked.

That is worth saying plainly up front, because "automating your quotes" sounds like a system you would have to buy and learn. Most of the result comes from four decisions about what a page says. The software part is small, optional, and mostly about what happens after someone raises their hand.

## Step one: find out what you are actually being asked

Open the last thirty messages you received from people who were not already clients, and make tally marks.

You are counting questions, not conversations. How many asked what something costs. How many asked whether you do a specific thing. How many asked where you are, whether you travel, or how far out you are booked.

Almost everyone who does this finds four or five questions covering most of their inbox. Those are not annoyances. They are the specification for your page, written by your own customers, and they beat any template.

The rule worth carrying: **any question you have answered more than twice is a section you have not written yet.**

## Step two: give every repeated question a stated answer

Take each question from your tally and write the answer the way you would say it out loud to somebody standing in front of you. Not marketing language. The actual sentence.

"I do knotless braids, box braids, and locs. I do not do color."
"I work Tuesday through Saturday, and evenings if you ask."
"I come to you anywhere inside the county; past that there is a travel fee."

Each one retires a whole category of message. Written once, they answer while you are with a client and while you are asleep — which is the only form of automation most small businesses actually need.

## Step three: attach a condition to every number

This is the step that makes posting a price safe. A number on its own can be quoted back at you by someone whose job looks nothing like the one you priced. A number with its condition attached cannot, because the condition travels with it.

Three shapes work, in ascending order of how much your work varies:

1. **A floor.** "Starting at $X." The smallest honest answer, and enough to tell a stranger which conversation they are in.
2. **A range with its variable named.** "Between $X and $Y, depending on length and density." The reader places themselves inside it, and the person who lands at the top already understands why.
3. **The thing that determines the price.** "Priced by square footage and condition." Use this when you genuinely cannot post a figure. Naming what the number depends on is still an answer, and a better one than silence.

Write the condition in the same breath as the number. A reader who has to hunt for the asterisk will assume the worst version of it.

## Step four: build the form around the variable

Some people will still need a real quote. The form is for them, and its job is narrow: collect the thing that determines the price, and nothing else.

If your price depends on length and density, ask for length and density. If it depends on square footage, ask for square footage. A photo upload is often worth more than four text fields, because it answers what the person asking does not know how to describe.

Then stop. Every extra field costs you completed forms, and a field you will not read costs you completed forms for nothing. ## What "automation" honestly means here

Now the word, defined plainly, because it gets used to mean four different things.

**Automation** just means a computer doing a step you would otherwise do by hand, on a rule you set. Here it is not intelligence and it is not a robot answering for you. It is three unglamorous things: the person who submits the form immediately sees a message saying it arrived and when you will reply, you get a notification wherever you actually look, and the inquiry lands somewhere you can still find it next Tuesday.

That is the whole of what most service businesses need automated at this stage. The confirmation message is the one that matters most — it is the difference between a person who waits for you and a person who messages the next name on their list.

## What this does not do

It does not increase how many people find you — that is a different problem with a different mechanism. It does not stop the conversations that should be conversations. And it will not survive contact with prices you have not actually decided; a page cannot state a range you have never committed to in your own head.

What it does is stop the same four questions from arriving forty times.
