Plenty of good businesses run entirely off a Facebook page. Customers message them, they reply, work gets booked. If that's you, the question isn't whether Facebook works — clearly it does. The question is what happens when it stops.

## A Facebook page is rented ground

You don't own it. You occupy it under terms someone else writes and can change without telling you.

Three things follow from that.

**The algorithm decides who sees you.** Organic reach for business pages has been falling for a decade. Your posts reach a fraction of the people who chose to follow you, and that fraction isn't yours to control. A website doesn't have an algorithm between you and the person who typed your name into Google.

**The rules can change tomorrow.** Layouts change, features get retired, and pages get flagged by automated systems with no warning and no useful appeal. It's rare. It's also completely outside your control, and the recovery process is a support queue.

**People check, then leave.** A customer who finds you on Facebook and wants to know your hours, your service area, and whether you cover their specific problem has to scroll a feed to find out. Most won't. They'll open a new tab and check the next business.

## What people expect when they're about to spend money

There's a difference between how someone browses and how someone buys.

Browsing happens on social. Buying involves checking. Before a stranger pays you for roof work, a haircut, or a catered event, they want to confirm you're a real business — that you have a proper site, that your details are consistent, that someone answers when they get in touch.

Not having a website doesn't read as "modern." It reads as "possibly not around next year." That's unfair, and it's still what people think.

## What you're actually missing

**Search.** Nobody Googles "plumber near me" and gets Facebook posts. Your competitors with websites are showing up for searches you're not in at all.

**A place to send people.** Every quote, invoice, business card, van, and yard sign should point somewhere. A page that requires people to be logged into a specific platform is a worse destination than a URL that works for everyone.

**Something you can prove.** When a customer asks what you do, "look at my Facebook" makes them work. "It's all on my site" doesn't.

## Where Google fits in

If you take one thing from this, make it this: a free Google Business Profile and a website are not alternatives. They work together, and most small businesses have neither set up properly.

Your Business Profile is the box that appears on the right of search results and pins you on Maps. It's free, it takes an afternoon, and for a local business it's usually the single highest-return thing you can do online. It's also where your reviews live, and reviews are what turn a search result into a phone call.

But a Business Profile has a field for your website, and what you put in it matters. Sending people to a Facebook page from there is a weaker result than sending them to a site with your services laid out. The profile gets you found. The site does the convincing.

The two also reinforce each other in search. Consistent name, address, and phone number across your profile, your site, and any directory listing is one of the clearest signals you can send that you're a real, findable business.

## What it doesn't mean

It doesn't mean quitting Facebook. Your page is genuinely useful — it's where regulars find you and where word of mouth travels. Keep posting.

The point is that it shouldn't be the only thing. Social is where people find you. Your site is where they decide. The two do different jobs, and the second one is the one you own.
